PPG Vitals Monitor

A pulse sensor that clips onto your fingertip. Light passes from an LED into a photodiode, and the it detects heartbeat through changes in the intensity of light passing through the finger.

Analog signal chain

There are three seperate circuits: The sensor circuit with the photodiode, TIA, and filters; the voltage divider, and the LED drive circuit.

Sensor Circuit: Photodiode + TIA + filters

This is the primary circuit in the project. It is broken up into the following stages:

  1. Photodiode (BPW34): The light sensor that enables the entire project. Converts the light recieved into a tiny current of about ~100 µA. Fluctuations from one's heartbeat is about 1µA.
  2. Transimpedance Amplifier (TIA): Amplifies the small current from the photodiode into a measurable voltage.
    This relies on an op-amp (MCP6002) and a feedback resistor set to 10 kΩ, giving a gain of 10 kV/A. With an input of 100µA, the output voltage will be 1 V.
  3. Low-pass filter: Removes high-frequency noise from the signal.
    RC filter with fc = 48 Hz. It uses a 330 Ω resistor and a 10 µF capacitor.
  4. High-pass filter: Removes the baseline signal so only the changing heartbeat signal remains.
    RC filter with fc = 0.32 Hz. It uses a 100 µF capacitor and a 5 kΩ resistor.
  5. Inverting amplifier: Amplifies the heartbeat signal to a level that can be read
    This uses an op-amp (MCP6002) with a 2 kΩ input resistor and a 100 kΩ feedback resistor, giving a gain of -50 V/V.
  6. Microcontroller: The signal is then fed into a Teensy 4.0 microcontroller to be processed

LED Drive Circuit

A simple circuit to drive the LED at a constant current. It is just the LED (TSAL6200) attatched to a 200 Ω resistor.

Voltage Divider

The design of my circuit requires voltages that range from -1.65V to 1.65V for the inputs of the opamps. As the microcontroller does not provide negative voltages, I used a voltage divider to create a virtual ground at the midpoint. The virtual ground is at 1.65V, while the high and low are 0V and 3.3V, which are -1.65V and 1.65V relative to the virtual ground.

The voltage divider consists of two resistors in series, with the midpoint connected to the virtual ground. It also requires a buffer to isolate the divider from the load. The buffer is a simple op-amp configured as a voltage follower.

PCB Design

After drawing the schematic, I laid out a two-layer PCB in KiCad. The board has an L-shaped outline to fit inside the enclosure, with the Teensy 4.0 and voltage reference on one end and the analog signal chain across the other.

Layout and routing

The photodiode produces a very small current, so I placed it close to the transimpedance amplifier and kept their connection short. From there, the signal passes through the low-pass filter, high-pass filter, and gain stage before reaching the Teensy. A ground copper pour covers the board around the routed traces.

Open full-size layout image

From the layout to the built board

I checked the component placement in KiCad's 3D view before having the board fabricated and soldering the parts by hand. The photodiode is mounted on the underside so it can face the finger channel when the board is installed in the enclosure.

  • Short sensor connectionThe photodiode sits beside the amplifier input to limit noise pickup before the signal is amplified.
  • Room for assemblyThrough-hole passives, DIP op-amps, and Teensy headers make the board possible to assemble by hand.
  • Optical alignmentMounting the photodiode on the back lets it look into the finger channel below the board.

Firmware and Python Script

The Teensy 4.0 reads the amplified sensor signal and sends each ADC sample to a computer over USB serial. A Python script then shows the live waveform and its frequency spectrum, making it easier to check the signal and estimate heart rate.

Sampling on the Teensy

The firmware reads pin A0 at 250 samples per second and prints one reading per line at 115200 baud. Subtracting the elapsed sample period keeps a delayed loop iteration from shifting the schedule of every later sample.

ppg_firmware/src/main.cpp · PlatformIO / Arduino

if (sinceLastSample >= SAMPLE_PERIOD_US) {
  sinceLastSample -= SAMPLE_PERIOD_US;

  int sample = analogRead(PPG_SIGNAL_PIN);
  Serial.println(sample);
}

Plotting and heart-rate estimate

The Python tool reads the serial stream with pyserial and plots a waveform and FFT spectrum with Matplotlib. It removes the average level, applies a Hann window, and finds the strongest frequency from 0.5 to 3.5 Hz. Multiplying that frequency by 60 gives an estimated BPM.

ppg_firmware/tools/live_plot.py · NumPy / Matplotlib / pyserial

detrended = np.array(sample_values) - np.mean(sample_values)
windowed = detrended * np.hanning(len(detrended))
mag = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(windowed))
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(len(windowed), d=1 / fs)

band = (freqs >= HR_BAND_HZ[0]) & (freqs <= HR_BAND_HZ[1])
if band.any():
    peak_freq = freqs[band][np.argmax(mag[band])]

3D Enclosure

I designed the enclosure in FreeCAD to hold the PCB and keep the LED and photodiode aligned across the finger channel. The L-shaped board sits in a matching bay, while the dark printed housing helps shield the sensor from room light.

Four printed parts

The housing is split into a main body, a removable cover, and separate holders for the LED and photodiode. Keeping the optics in their own pieces made it possible to position them on opposite sides of the finger.

Printed and assembled

After printing the parts, I test-fitted the assembled board inside the enclosure. The photodiode faces into the finger channel through an opening in the housing.
